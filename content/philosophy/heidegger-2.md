> [1] Heidegger's distinction between 'conspicuousness' (Auffalligkeit')
> 'obtrusiveness' ('Aufdringlichkeit'), and 'obstinacy' ('Aufsässigkeit') is
> hard to present unambiguously in translation. He seems to have in mind three
> rather similar situations. In each of these we are confronted by a number of
> articles which are ready-to-hand. In the first situation we wish to use one of
> these articles for some purpose, but we find that it cannot be used for that
> purpose. It then becomes 'conspicuous' or 'striking', and in a way
> 'un-ready-to-hand'—in that we are not able to use it. In the second situation
> we may have precisely the same articles before us, but we want one which is
> not there. In this case the missing article too is 'un-ready-to-hand', but in
> another way—in that it is not there to be used. This is annoying, and the
> articles which are still ready-to-hand before us, thrust themselves upon us in
> such a way that they become 'obtrusive' or even 'obnoxious'. In the third
> situation, some of the articles which are ready-to-hand before us are
> experienced as obstacles to the achievement of some purpose; as obstacles they
> are 'obstinate', 'recalcitrant', 'refractory', and we have to attend to them
> or dispose of them in some way before we can finish what we want to do. Here
> again the obstinate objects are un-ready-to-hand, but simply in the way of
> being obstinate.

[Source](https://www.beyng.com/pages/en/BeingandTimeMR/BeingandTimeMR.103.html)

I was musing over the notion of frames recently, and apparently Heidegger had a
very similar thought to me?

For some reason, I got back into thinking about what the `a priori` is. The
rationalists had "pure reason/intellect". Then we formalized it into
logic/mathematics in the 20th century. But these are mainly standards for, what
is true and how do we know it?

I think a priori could also be something like, what constitutes the very space
of thoughts you can have. Like, we can have discussions about the truth or
falseness of chair-like propositions, but that presupposes a chair. This isn't
new - a lot of the Logical Empiricists (Reichenbach, for example) discussed this
phenomenon. That's probably where the whole "true in virtue of meaning" came
from too (although the way that meaning was instantiated definitely did not live
up to the hype…).

You can point to this and maybe say, language is fucked. Or you can try and take
a fallibilist approach and allow questioning of everything. But apparently,
phenomenology has got me covered here for a more specific dynamic.

---

A working hypothesis I have is that intelligence requires the ability to
actively close off something and dogmatically assert a _frame_ (not just a true
statement) explicitly, while also having the ability to acknowledge that the
frame can be broken; BUT without resorting to fallibilsim or some kind of
higher-level re-descriptoin of how the frame is broken.

When I use encryption and the SHA256 model, for example, I can think:

- This is a secure hash.
- Unless Quantum Computing arrives.

Notice what _doesn't_ happen - I don't port above the "hey, 1/2^256 chance of
hash collision". Even though those things are objectively true. There's a
difference between the exported model that I even work with constantly, and the
"objective" way things work.

And to _further sink this in_, I am _explicitly not constantly reasoning down
the "bad path"_. Not like in a tree search kind of way, where it's even an if
statement to see if I go down that. No, at some point, it _does_ become dogma to
not search down that path.

In this sense, I don't believe that fallibilsm is an actually workable
philosophy, funnily enough. And also Bayesianism (as a philosophy) as well. If
your semantics require complete takeover of your space, then it's probably not
very actionable. And also Carnap's philosophy, etc. All of these
meta-philosophies which try and formally reduce everything to its ontology
doesn't really capture the nature of what it _means_ to be the "base" layer.
They also have the issue that I see all too common - you're going to provide the
universal standard substrate for other people? _that_, in it of itself, warrants
suspicion.

The Heidegger passage above is interesting because it actually gives an account
of how you break out of dogma. Well, maybe I'm being too fair to Heidegger ;
after all, you _could_ just reduce this to, "We can discover that we're wrong".
But I think it's the unique constraint that it's _dogma_. By definition, you
aren't supposed to be aware that it's an a priori thing or even that it, itself,
is dogma.

I don't think that these kinds of thoughts are useless, BTW. I mean, the CS
world has operationalized a very near-field thought: _correct by construction_.
If fallibilism is "model everything with Bayesianism", then correct by
construction is avoiding "bad states" because they're literally not
instantiatable. This is an operational model that has no woo-woo nonsense.

Admittedly, I haven't worked out the kinks of this idea, and I'm not trying to
become a Heidegger; I want to actually operationalize this somehow. Either in my
own life, or in machine learning.

The pragmatist tradition has some interesting reversals (in my mind, they're the
"reversal guys"), but they can commit some of the same errors that they claim
other traditions do (at least, Dewey/James is like that; Sellars is more
interesting.)
