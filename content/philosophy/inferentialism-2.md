## Inference and Meaning

So if Quine & Meaning Postulates are like attacking the notion of a "privileged
state space filter", Sellars takes another angle on Carnap that I did not know
because I am not a Carnap scholar (though, if I keep on sitting in 1950s
philosophy of language, I may become one...)

`P rules` versus `L rules` are basically: `L rules` are arbitrary rewrite rules
you can apply anywhere, whereas `P rules` are rewrite rules that are dependent
on the predicates that go into the rewrite; they are contextual. `P rules`,
similar to meaning postulates, are part of the framework, but the point is
is that they need outside justification to be a part of said framework.

Now, rather than take Quine's approach and attack this kind of "arbitrariness",
Sellars takes this and notices that, perhaps there's something _interesting_
here and valuable. Perhaps these `P rules` (not literally by the end; but the
intuition) are the things that constitute meaning.

Now personally, this is a genius observation. I mean, inferentialism, if you
actually try and understand what it's saying, is very unintuitive, and
completely opposite of normal intuitions (a chair is a chair because... it's
situated in the space of reasons???). But even the base intuition - I have
no idea how you'd think of _meaning_ as being constituted in terms of its
consequences, and only that.

Of course, it remains to _prove_ that we're actually onto something, and not
merely looking at one quirky part of Carnapian logic (e.g. Quine took
a completely different lesson from his dive into Carnap).

I'm personally split. It feels so obvious that, I say something is red because,
well, I see red, and I somehow, through psychology/biology, remember that it's
red, and through the combination of all of that, I can tell that something is
red.

But for something like a "Hydrogen atom", it's plain to me that this concept
is very much determined by its downstream implications between theories.
I mean, sure, we could try to make the one master definition of the Hydrogen
Atom and then keep revising it, but I think inferentialism here is a much
better account of what's going on. It unifies both the intuitive side and how
theory revision actually attempts to work.

Maybe empiricist concepts, ironically enough, work for simple, intuitive,
biological things, while higher-level concepts are needed for higher-level
things. Not sure why there is a need to privilege one absolute basis. But
admittedly, I haven't thought things through.

## Descriptivism

Despite all of this, I still don't really understand the "puzzles" around
descripitivism.

From the way I'm seeing it, it's the classic engineering problem - you set out
to solve a problem, but how do you "solve the problem?" You solve a proxy of
the problem.

Similarly, it's the "paradox" of how we have a mind-independent world, yet
because we are limited human beings, our way of interactign with the world is
necessarily insufficient compared to the world (not just language - this
applies to any endeavor).

That's how I'm viewing the central "issue" of descriptivism. It's not trying
to be Cartesian doubt or anything, it's trying to frame this as a central
concern and then "pick a side".

And inferentialism sidesteps this, and this "central issue" is most of what
Rorty says is _unproductive_ when privileged.

The only issue is that this isn't actually descriptivism. I brought up in a
previous post how this was sort of like trying to access a database element
by doing some werid ad-hoc query to isolate just said element (this resembles
a lot of tricks that Russel, and in general the "logical form" people used, for
example). I have this framing. But apparently the problem of `reference` goes
beyond this aspect - more specific? more general? Not sure.
