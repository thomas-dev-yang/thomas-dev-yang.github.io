I read `Drive` by Daniel Pink a decade ago, in high school. Since then, my
framework for getting shit done has been intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation.

This lined up decently well for a long time. But there were cracks
in the idea - I had enough "intrinsic motivation" to keep going, I guess? But
overall I think the whole framework is unsustainable.

This framing tries to understand your set of actions as:

- the "good set": intrinsic
- the "bad set": extrinsic

The idea that I took away from it was, you need to find actions better aligned
with this thing called "intrinsic motivation" and less in line with this thing
called "extrinsic motivation".

I'm cutting orthogonally to that. I want to explore this two part systems of
Goals and Garbage:

- Somehow, you must have a strong goal that you keep your mind towards.
- There must be "garbage work" in said goals.

## Goals

"Goal" can mean anything. It can mean this thing we call "passion". It could
mean an external paycheck. It can mean something as generic as survival (and
we interpret this in a Darwinian sense). "Goals" are not this objective,
defined thing.

But I think it's deeper than that. I guess it's this sense of permanence, that
comes from _whatever source it comes from_, that is the real thing you're
anchoring to. "Goal" is a contract, rational, or irrational, that you _feel_
like you never have to question.

I say it like this, because I'm thinking about capacities where I can offload
the ambiguous mental stress of thinking about ambiguous goals, and a canonical
example is work.

I would say it's not _merely_ because my tasks are "well defined" (relative to
some of the huge scope that the company has), but also stuff like, "somebody else
has figured this out", "even if the company fails, I can find another job",
stuff like that. In this sense, good goals put up good boundaries.

I was trying to reconcile this, because this makes goals seem a bit negative.
What about goals that are "intrinsic"? I think that what these goals let you
get away with is a justification (to yourself, at the very least) to ignore
certain constraints. It's almost like a self-limiting tool, in order to spike
in one area. At least for me, that's the motto I've usually held in my head -
I'm _so passionate_ that I am _willing to ignore XYZ_.

## Garbage

So, if you have a goal, one with this "permanence" aspect, and obviously
some force (positive or negative) wanting it to succeed, well, you have your
work cut out for you. Not really - the point of "garbage" is navigating the
ambiguity, sucking it up, and pulling through. But navigating ambiguity is
usually done _for_ something.

## Garbage... with no goal

This is the killer that we want to address. For personal learnings with
"no stakes", I've never been able to get it to succeed, for this reason.

Well, sure - I'd pull up a blog, read some source code, and genuinely, my
sporadic, one-off learnings has helped on the job before. I've read up on
Python internals back in sophomore year of college, and on two jobs, funnily
enough, did I have to deal with objects at the PyObject level. But I'm not
a Python core maintainer, or a maintainer of any library.

I could set up a study group, or force myself to release a concrete artifact,
force myself to stick to one project for 6 months, sit down and read a textbook
front to back, or something else. That's what the classic sayings are. It's
always, anchor yourself to _one_ thing.

But on the job, there have been _plenty_ of times where, on the same project,
we've pivoted completely different directions. And a lot of my high-leverage
knowledge has been the _accidental_ knowledge that I learned on the quest for
a very concrete goal (no matter how artificial/BS it was!). I learned quite
about the Linux kernel from a project at my job that, while needed to be done,
kind of was BS long term. I've learned a lot about math while doing random
things here and there, and fun math - I thought all statistics and linear
algebra was really boring, but I never thought that it'd actuall be useful
in signal processing of all places.

Arguably, I'd say I had _far more clarity_ when I had a specific goal I wanted
to extract out of a field, and explicitly said, "This doesn't sound useful".
A naive interpretation of "intrinsic motivation" _would_ say that this is not
"correct" behavior, and yet this kind of behavior has lead to some of my
highest leverage bits of knowledge.

However, translating that kind of high-level drive to a personal project, inflates
into ridiculous things like, starting a side business, or becoming an OSS maintainer.
Then you get the same "ambiguous pressures" as a job, that incentives you to _do
whatever it takes_.

(Of course, I also believe a "passion side project" could work here, but
I haven't found anything that scratched my itch, in a deep way to "keep this
going", as opposed to "this is a cool technology that I wanna explore for a few
days.")

## Harnesses & Manufacturing Passion

This is more of a coping mechanism than a real, positive philosophy. There are
plenty of things in life that we don't want to do, but we must do. And not in
a "Ultimately in service to a greater goal" kind of way - you could interpret
this statement as "Good goal, have to do garbage"; no, I mean that the goal
itself, you don't believe in either. A trivial example we all can relate to is
the education system. Or a boring job.

In this situation, one thing I would always do is, find something interesting
to do, and laser-focus on that, within the system. In this sense, this is a
_manufacturing_ of a goal.

Another bias to watch out for is the bias of "one". An easy example is existing
harnesses - video games, structures that incentivize you to continue engaging
with it, etc. These are not structures that (you usually) consciously create.
Either someone else has done it, or it's something that people have accepted
and settle into, etc. It's easy to call out these kinds of structures (even
if it's not easy to escape them!)

It's harder to get rid of this bias when _creating_ your own harnesses. A
thing I tell myself is, "I'm gonna sit down, and understand every part of
this textbook!" That's not how learning works. The issue is, you see this
"one" product, and you extrapolate some essential structure from it. Yes, it
is a stable capacity; yes, it's gone through all the heuristics you can name
of. But no, it doesn't mean that it's a _harness_ to actually help bootstrap
you.

> Note: This actually _is_ how it worked for a philosophy of science textbook,
> but only after I've spent a few months digging through adjacent literature
> and thinking through the related problems myself. In that case, I consumed
> the entire textbook in less than 8 hours during a lazy day at work, and it
> helped synthesize and reformulate a lot of my thoughts - but only because
> I've already been primed for the subject. _Theory and Reality_ by Peter
> Godfrey-Smith - check it out.

It's not enough to just say "Everything is patchwork" though. I've embraced
said philosophy, and while it's been helpful for exploring new things, when
done wrong, it obviously leads to unstructured meandering.

And so we're back at the, "Don't overthink, just do _one_ little thing"
approach. Which honestly, is pretty good. This blog wouldn't be written without
such a heuristic.

It's this careful balance of not optimizing too hard immediately for this
abstract "essential structure", yet still manufacturing it properly, with the
meta-knowledge that you _are_ manufacturing it.
